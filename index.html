How this works, in short

For each area, we look at how much rain falls in summer, how many days are free of frost, how hot July gets, and how windy spring is. These come from NASA POWER, a free NASA service that turns satellite and weather-model data into daily weather records.

We then check each rotation plan against that weather: does the crop get enough water, will it finish before frost, does it build the soil, does it keep the ground covered in windy months, can it earn money, and does it feed animals? Your answers decide which of these count most.

How sure can you be?

Weather changes a lot from year to year. That is why you can test a dry year and a short, hot season, not just an average year. Each plan also shows the lowest and highest rain and frost-free days NASA recorded since 2001.

The plans are compared with simple rules you can check, not a hidden computer model. We chose this so farmers and agronomists can see exactly why a plan comes first and disagree with it where their experience says otherwise.

Satellite data and your own eyes

NASA data covers large areas, about 55 × 40 km for each number here, and the same way every year, everywhere. That makes it good for seeing long-term patterns like dry years and frost dates. It cannot see your field's slope, a wet hollow or a windbreak.

What you see on the ground is the opposite: very detailed, but only for one place. The best decisions use both. If your field is often drier, frostier or windier than your neighbours', give that more weight than our numbers.

Words to know

Crop rotation
Growing different crops on the same field in turn, year after year, instead of the same crop every year.
Fallow / rest year
A year with no crop. A bare (clean) fallow is cultivated to kill weeds. A stubble fallow keeps last year's stubble standing.
Days without frost
The days between the last frost in spring and the first frost in autumn. A crop must grow and ripen inside this window.
Nitrogen-fixing crop
A crop like peas whose roots take nitrogen from the air and leave some in the soil for the next crop.
Evapotranspiration
Water leaving the field as vapour, from the soil surface and through crop leaves. Hot, windy days raise it.
Water balance
Rain plus stored soil water, minus what crops and the soil lose. If it goes negative, crops are short of water.
Soil moisture anomaly
How much wetter or drier the soil is than normal for that time of year. NASA's SMAP satellite measures this.
NDVI
A satellite measure of how green and leafy a place is, from 0 (bare soil) to about 0.9 (thick green crop).
Wind erosion
Wind blowing away loose, dry topsoil, the richest layer. It is worst on bare fields in April and May.
"1 summer in 4"
Our dry-year rain: in a quarter of the years NASA data covers (since 2001), summer rain was this low or lower.

Details for experts: scoring formulas and crop values

Inputs. R = summer rain (dry-year value if a dry year is chosen). F = days without frost (−12 for a short, hot season). T = July average (+1.5°C for a short, hot season).

Water. Mean over the years of the cycle. Crop year: 100 × clamp(((R + carry) ÷ D − 0.45) ÷ 0.55), D = crop water need, carry = soil water left by the crop before (60 mm after a bare rest year, 80 mm after a stubble rest year, 10–15 mm after pulses, −15 to −20 mm after sunflower or alfalfa). Rest year: 85. Watering adds 150 mm. Soil: sandy holds less (carry × 0.6, −20 mm), heavy holds more (carry × 1.2, +10 mm).

Frost and heat. Per crop year: clamp(40 + 2 × (F − need)), minus 12 × max(0, T − 18.5) for heat-sensitive crops. Plan = half the worst year + half the average.

Soil. Mean of the crops' soil values, −10 when a crop follows one of its own family. Animal feed. Mean of the crops' feed values. Ground cover. 100 × (1 − weighted bare months ÷ (10 × years)); bare Oct–Mar months count 1, bare Apr–May months count 2; × 1.3 on sandy soil, × 0.85 on heavy soil. Income. Mean of a relative market value (rest year = 0); not a price forecast.

Match. Weighted average. Low = 0, Medium = 3, High = 5. Frost and heat always weigh 2. Water weighs double in a dry year. Words: Good ≥ 70, Fair 45–69, Weak < 45.
